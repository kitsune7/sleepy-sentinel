Section 1The decision week: what Assignment 8 actually asks

Assignment 7 ended on a cliffhanger, and it's worth replaying it precisely, because everything this week does follows from it. The full-feature logistic model had just become the champion — QWK 0.481 against PERCLOS's 0.410 — and when we opened the box to see why, the coefficients pointed at one family: blink dynamics. Not yawns, not head pose. Eyelids. And that observation carried an uncomfortable implication. Every feature the model sees describes what the eyes did inside one 15-second window. If the real signal is how blink behavior evolves over minutes, our table of tidy per-window rows has been quietly throwing it away.

Assignment 8 is the week the course hands you time to resolve exactly this kind of question. The professor's framing is unusually explicit about what it is not: not a requirement to fine-tune anything, not a model tournament, not an invitation to adopt whatever Week 8's lectures covered. It asks for a decision memo in three parts — where the project stands (Part A), a revised modeling strategy with alternatives explicitly considered and rejected (Part B), and an updated risk picture plus a handoff to Week 9's failure analysis (Part C). A small experiment is optional, permitted only if it directly serves the decision.

Ours does, twice over. A7 left a prioritized question — does temporal modeling over the existing window sequences beat the champion, with no new data? — and there are two natural ways to ask it, which bracket the answer nicely:

Contender 1 · trend_logistic
Hand-built temporal features
Keep the champion's linear model. Append causal cross-window trends (delta, slope, drift, elapsed time). If temporal signal is simple, this catches it cheaply and interpretably.
Contender 2 · gru_sequence
A small sequence model
Let a small GRU read the whole video, window by window, and learn temporal structure itself — the bounded echo of the dataset authors' own HM-LSTM baseline.

Running both is not a tournament; it's a designed contrast. If only the trend features win, the temporal signal is real but simple. If only the GRU wins, the signal is real but subtle — trends were too crude. If neither wins, the representation wasn't the bottleneck after all, and the decision memo says so. Whatever happens, Part B gets its answer with evidence rather than vibes.

Key idea · A decision needs a bracket One experiment tells you whether a specific model wins. Two experiments that bracket a hypothesis — simplest-possible and flexible-possible — tell you something about the hypothesis itself. That's what turns this week's runs into Part B evidence instead of leaderboard noise.

One more piece of table-setting. The user answered a design question early: the MLP's known feature-path bug from A7 (it silently consumed the four luminance confound columns) stays documented but unfixed, because the MLP is not on the recommended path and the deeper issue — too little data for the capacity — wouldn't change. Both of this week's new models select their features through the charter-correct path that excludes luminance, so the bug touches nothing we run. Keep that in mind in Section 5, where protocol hygiene becomes the whole story.

Section 2The suspect: what a per-window table throws away

To see what the tabularization discards, you don't need theory — you need one real video. Meet 05/10: subject 05's drowsy-labeled session, 79 windows long. Here is its perclos (fraction of time the eyes are closed) across the session, window by window:

For the first six minutes — 48 windows — this drowsy person's eyes are essentially open: perclos hovers near 0.00, indistinguishable from their own alert video, where it never exceeds 0.007. Then the fight begins, and it comes in waves: a burst of microsleeps around window 50 (perclos 0.696), a brief recovery, a worse wave (0.878, then 0.904), another recovery where the eyes stay open for a full minute — and a final collapse in the last three windows, where blink_dur_max records a single eye closure lasting 8.4 seconds. Fighting sleep looks exactly like this: losing, rallying, losing again.

Now put yourself in the shoes of the A7 champion. It scores every window independently and averages the probabilities. Window 40 of this video — perclos 0.000, ordinary blinks — looks exactly like an alert window, because in isolation it is one. So do the recovery windows between the waves. The champion has no way to know that these quiet windows sit inside a session that is repeatedly collapsing, or that window 50's spike is a dramatic departure from this person's own six-minute baseline rather than, say, someone who always squints. And the wave structure itself — collapse, rally, worse collapse — is invisible to a model that never sees two windows together. Every row is an orphan.

Definition · The temporal hypothesis The claim under test this week: fatigue is visible not only in the level of eyelid features within a window, but in their change across windows — departures from the recent past, sustained slopes, and drift away from the session's own starting baseline. The per-window table cannot express any of these, because each row sees only itself.

There are two distinct ways this could matter, and they map onto our two contenders. First, the change signals might be simple — literally "current value minus what's been typical so far." A linear model could use that, if only someone computed the feature. Second, the signal might be structural — long-range patterns in ordering, like microsleep episodes growing more frequent, which no fixed formula captures but a recurrent network might learn. And notice who has taken the second bet before: the dataset's own authors published an HM-LSTM over sequential blink features as their baseline model. Our GRU is the bounded, honest-sized version of that bet.

Why should the middle class, specifically, benefit? Recall the standing failure: every model to date — PERCLOS, MLP, champion — recalls low-vigilant at 14/60 or worse, essentially chance. The per-window view offers a plausible reason. A low-vigilant person's individual windows look like mild versions of both neighbors: slightly longer blinks than alert, but nothing like a drowsy collapse. On a level scale they sit in an ambiguous band. But on a change scale, they might be distinctive: elevated relative to their own session start, yet stable — not the runaway deterioration of 05/10's finale. If that's true, trend features should specifically help the class we most need. That is a prediction we can check in Section 6.

Edge case · Windows are already "temporal" — within themselves Don't confuse the two timescales. Features like blink_rate already summarize 15 seconds of dynamics — that's within-window time, and the champion has it. What's missing is across-window time: minutes-scale evolution. A8's question is only about the second timescale.

Section 3Contender one: trend features that cannot see the future

The first contender changes nothing about the model and everything about what it sees. The champion stays: median-impute, standardize, balanced multinomial logistic regression, window probabilities averaged per video. We simply widen each row. Alongside the 18 per-window features, every window gains a description of how it compares to its own session's past — and only its past.

Three kinds of comparison, computed for each of the nine eyelid-dynamics features that A7's coefficients flagged (perclos, blink rate, blink durations, eye-blink stats, EAR stats):

delta
current value − mean of the previous 8 windows (~1 min of past). "Am I suddenly different from just now?"
slope
least-squares slope over the trailing 8 windows. "Am I trending, and which way?"
drift
current value − expanding mean of all earlier windows this session. "How far have I moved from my own baseline?"
elapsed_min
minutes since session start (one shared column). "How deep into the session are we?"

That's 9 features × 3 trends + 1 = 28 new columns, giving 46 in total. Restricting trends to the eyelid cluster is deliberate: at 60 subjects, every added column is a statistical liability, so the trends go only where the evidence already pointed. Here is the heart of the implementation, in the new src/training/temporal.py:

for col in TREND_COLUMNS:
    series = result[col]
    # mean of the PREVIOUS 8 windows — shift(1) excludes the current one
    past_mean = grouped[col].transform(
        lambda s: s.shift(1).rolling(TREND_PAST_WINDOWS, min_periods=1).mean()
    )
    # expanding mean of ALL earlier windows — the session's own baseline
    session_baseline = grouped[col].transform(lambda s: s.expanding().mean().shift(1))

    result[f"{col}_delta"] = (series - past_mean).fillna(0.0)
    result[f"{col}_slope"] = grouped[col].transform(_trailing_slopes)
    result[f"{col}_drift"] = (series - session_baseline).fillna(0.0)

result["elapsed_min"] = result["window_idx"] * STRIDE_SEC / 60.0

Two details in that snippet carry all the safety. The grouped object is a groupby on video_id, so no computation ever crosses from one video into another — subject 05's drowsy session can't borrow baseline from subject 05's alert session, let alone another subject's. And the .shift(1) calls push every history window one step back, so the "past" genuinely excludes the present and everything after it.

Definition · Causal feature A feature at window t is causal if it can be computed from windows 0..t alone. A deployed early-fatigue aid runs live — at the moment it must score you, the future hasn't happened. Any feature that peeks ahead would make offline scores unachievable in deployment: a quiet form of leakage, along the time axis instead of the subject axis.

Causality is exactly the kind of property that silently breaks during refactoring, so it isn't left to code review. A unit test corrupts the late windows of a video and asserts that every trend feature of the early windows is bit-for-bit unchanged. If someone ever swaps a shift(1) for a centered window, the suite fails:

def test_trend_features_are_causal() -> None:
    """Editing a later window must never change trend features at earlier windows."""
    original = temporal.add_trend_features(windows_df)

    corrupted_input = windows_df.copy()
    late = (corrupted_input["video_id"] == "01/0") & (corrupted_input["window_idx"] >= 8)
    corrupted_input.loc[late, list(temporal.TREND_COLUMNS)] = 99.0
    corrupted = temporal.add_trend_features(corrupted_input)

    early = original["window_idx"] < 8
    pd.testing.assert_frame_equal(original.loc[early, trend_cols], corrupted.loc[early, trend_cols])

Edge case · The cold start At window 0 there is no past, so delta, slope, and drift are all defined as 0.0 — the trend features go silent and the model falls back on level features alone, i.e. it behaves like the A7 champion until about a minute of history accumulates. Graceful — but it plants a deeper question: what happens to drift if a user starts a session already drowsy? Their baseline is drowsy, so drift ≈ 0 exactly where it should scream. Hold this thought; it becomes a headline risk in Section 7.

Section 4Contender two: a GRU that reads the whole session

The second contender abandons hand-crafting entirely. If minutes-scale structure matters, the purist's move is to hand a recurrent network the raw sequence of window features — all ~79 rows of a video, in order — and let it learn whatever temporal patterns exist. One video in, one label out.

A GRU (gated recurrent unit) reads a sequence step by step, maintaining a hidden state — think of it as a small, learned working memory. At each window it decides, via learned gates, how much of the old memory to keep and how much of the new window to absorb. In principle this can represent everything our trend features encode and anything richer: growing episode frequency, alternation patterns, recovery-and-relapse shapes. The version here is deliberately small and matches the MLP's regularization conventions: hidden size 32, dropout 0.25, weight decay 1e-4, Adam at 1e-3.

class GruVideoClassifier(torch.nn.Module):
    """Small many-to-one GRU: read a video's window sequence, predict its label."""

    def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dim=32, dropout=0.25, num_classes=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru = torch.nn.GRU(input_dim, hidden_dim, batch_first=True)
        self.dropout = torch.nn.Dropout(dropout)
        self.head = torch.nn.Linear(hidden_dim, num_classes)

    def forward(self, padded, lengths):
        packed = torch.nn.utils.rnn.pack_padded_sequence(
            padded, lengths.cpu(), batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False
        )
        _, final_hidden = self.gru(packed)
        return self.head(self.dropout(final_hidden[-1]))

The fiddly part is that videos have different lengths — 50 to 135 windows. To batch them, shorter sequences are zero-padded up to the longest in the batch, and a lengths tensor records each video's true length. pack_padded_sequence then tells the GRU where each real sequence ends, so the recurrence simply stops there: the padding is never read, and the "final" hidden state is the state at each video's true last window, not at the padded end. This, too, is unit-tested — a sequence scored alone must produce bit-identical logits to the same sequence sharing a batch with a longer one.

Key idea · The bet the GRU is making With LOSO, each fold trains on 59 subjects minus 9 validation subjects — about 150 videos. A GRU asks the data to teach it gates, memory dynamics, and a classifier from 150 labeled sequences. The trend-feature contender asks the data only to weight 46 pre-built columns. Same information, wildly different statistical asks. A6 already showed what happens when capacity outruns data (the MLP lost to one feature); the GRU re-poses that question at the sequence level, deliberately.

Everything around the model follows the established protocol. Features are median-imputed and standardized by a preprocessor fit on training-fold windows only, then reshaped into per-video sequences. The 9 validation subjects carved out within each fold (the same ones recorded in folds.json) drive early stopping on video-level validation QWK with patience 8, exactly as the MLP did in A5/A6. Class weights are computed from training-video label counts. Seeds follow the 42 + fold convention. Sixty folds, each producing three test-video predictions — the identical shape every previous model produced, so every downstream table and metric works unchanged.

Section 5The referee: saved folds and the anchor run

Before any result can be read, a quieter question has to be settled: are this week's numbers comparable to last week's? Three assignments of evidence — every claim in the memo about "improves on A7" or "loses to the champion" — rest on the comparison being apples-to-apples. Two mechanisms guarantee it.

First, the folds are loaded, not regenerated. A7 saved the complete subject-to-fold assignment — including which 9 subjects serve as validation carve-outs in each fold — to outputs/folds.json. The new runner reads that file. Same 60 folds, same held-out subject per fold, same validation subjects for the GRU's early stopping that the MLP used. Nothing about the exam changed; only the students taking it.

Second — and this is the belt to that suspender — the runner re-executes the A7 champion itself as a fourth run, logistic_full_anchor. It's the exact same spec (same features, same pipeline, same seed + fold convention) pushed through this week's code path. If the anchor's 60 fold-level QWK values match A7's stored fold_metrics.csv exactly, then the loaded folds, the preprocessing, the aggregation, and the metric code are all provably unchanged — and every difference we observe in the new models is attributable to the models. If even one fold drifted, something in the harness moved and no comparison is safe.

Key idea · Reproduce before you compare An anchor run converts "we believe nothing changed" into "we verified nothing changed." It costs a few seconds of compute and catches an entire category of silent errors: environment drift, refactoring side-effects, fold-file corruption, accidental seed changes. This week it passed — all 60 folds match A7 to machine precision (and did so even running under different library versions than the original).

It's worth pausing on why LOSO remains the right exam at all, because the temporal features raise a fresh leakage question. Each trend feature references other windows of the same video. Is that leakage? No — and the distinction matters. Leakage is information available at training/evaluation time that won't be available at prediction time. A live fatigue aid genuinely has the session's past windows (it has been watching you); it does not have your identity in its training set (subject-wise LOSO handles that) and does not have your future (causality, Section 3, handles that). The three protections cover three different axes:

Subject axis
LOSO folds + disjointness assertions: the test subject's windows never touch training.
Time axis
Causal trend features + unit test: no window reads its future.
Preprocessing axis
Imputer/scaler fit on training folds only, then applied to validation/test.

One practical note completes the picture. The experiments live in a new entry point — uv run train_temporal — rather than being bolted onto train_alertness. The A7 command and its outputs remain untouched and re-runnable; the new runner writes its own artifact family (temporal_*.csv, temporal_manifest.json) beside them. Comparisons should never require destroying the thing you're comparing against.

Why does it work? Open the box, as always. Standardized coefficient magnitudes, averaged over all 60 fold-refits:

The drift family dominates the newcomers — the session-baseline comparison, not the short-horizon delta or slope. And the signed coefficients tell an interpretable story about the middle class. For the low-vigilant class, blink-duration level pushes up (+1.56) while blink-duration drift pushes down (−1.30): the model has learned that low vigilance looks like long blinks that are not still worsening — elevated relative to an alert person, stable relative to the session's own trajectory. Runaway drift is what marks full drowsiness (think window 76 of 05/10). That "elevated but stable" signature is precisely the thing a per-window row could never express, which is a satisfying explanation for why every per-window model was stuck at 14/60.

And the GRU? It lost on every metric — QWK 0.361 ± 0.470, corners worsening to 25/180, low-vigilant recall 12/60, and a per-fold record of 14 wins, 24 ties, 22 losses against the anchor. The learning curves make the diagnosis unambiguous: early stopping fired at a median of epoch 6 out of a possible 40, meaning validation QWK peaked almost immediately and declined as the network kept fitting its ~150 training sequences. This is the A6 lesson — capacity is not the bottleneck — reconfirmed one level up, and it's the most useful kind of loss: the temporal signal is real (trend features prove it), but at n=60 subjects it must be handed to the model, not learned by it.

Key finding · Representation beat capacity, again Same information was available to both contenders. The one that encoded temporal structure as 28 fixed columns beat the champion where it mattered; the one that tried to learn temporal structure from 150 sequences overfit by epoch 6 and lost everywhere. In small-data regimes, knowledge you can write down beats flexibility you can't feed.

One caution before celebrating: confidence behavior. The gap between mean confidence on correct vs incorrect predictions widens (0.575/0.520, vs 0.489/0.446 for the anchor) — but it remains far too small to hang a warning threshold on. Calibration stays on the pre-deployment to-do list, unchanged since A6.

Section 7The decision: what we adopt, what we reject, and why

Part B is where the assignment stops being an experiment report and becomes a judgment. The judgment: adopt trend_logistic — frozen pretrained landmark extraction, per-window geometric summaries, causal cross-window trends, multinomial logistic regression — as the modeling path into Week 9 and the final package. One planned experiment survives from earlier weeks: the CORN ordinal head on this same representation, judged by the same bar, before the final package.

The assignment also demands verdicts on each course method, and this is where a quiet reframing happens. A7 called transfer learning "indirectly relevant" because MediaPipe is a pretrained model upstream. On reflection, that undersells it: frozen pretrained feature extraction is directly, centrally what this project does. The landmarker is a pretrained CNN whose outputs feed everything downstream. The project was never a counterexample to Week 7/8's material — it's an instance of it, with task-specific geometric outputs in place of generic embeddings. What remains rejected is the rest of the menu:

Key idea · A rejection with evidence beats a rejection with vibes A7 rejected sequence models on reasoning (too little data, probably). A8 rejected them on measurement (ran one, watched it overfit, counted the folds). The conclusion is the same; its standing in the final portfolio is completely different. That upgrade — preliminary decision to evidenced decision — is the entire point the assignment's "what changed since Week 7" question is probing.

Honest decisions also carry their price tags, and trend_logistic introduces three real risks. First, the cold start: trend features are zeros until history accumulates, so for the first minute the model is effectively the A7 champion — fine, but it must be stated in the deployment context. Second, and more seriously, the session-start assumption: drift measures change from the session's own beginning. A user who sits down already exhausted has an exhausted baseline — drift reads ≈ 0 precisely where it should be loudest. The dataset cannot surface this failure because every video carries one constant label; there are no sessions that start drowsy and are labeled by moment. Third, the mild variance cost: 46 features against 60 subjects widens the QWK fold band slightly (±0.399 vs ±0.378). Against these, the strategy reduces the project's biggest standing risk — middle-class blindness — and adds zero new dependencies, data, or licensing surface: the champion path remains sklearn-only, torch stays optional for diagnostics.

Edge case · The blind spot the dataset can't see Every UTA-RLDD video has one label for its whole duration, so "session that begins drowsy" exists in the data — but "drift from baseline" within such a session is drift from a drowsy baseline. The model may lean on a crutch ("everyone starts near their alert baseline") that real deployments will kick away. Week 9's failure analysis inherits this as a named question, with a concrete probe: score truncated sessions that begin mid-video inside drowsy recordings and measure how much drowsy recall depends on having seen an alert-ish start.

Section 8Part C, the Week 9 handoff, and what you're turning in

Part C asks a narrow question: did the revised strategy change the risk profile recorded in the dataset audit? Mostly no — same data, same upstream extractor, same licenses, no fine-tuning, no new privacy surface. But two assumptions genuinely moved, and one process improvement is worth recording:

  • Prediction-time inputs
    The model's input is no longer "the current window" but "the current window plus session history." The audit now assumes continuous sessions, a near-baseline start, and ~1 minute of warm-up — with graceful degradation to champion behavior when history is absent.
  • Representativeness
    Drift presumes the session start approximates a personal baseline. Sessions started mid-fatigue violate it silently — and whether drift trajectories differ across demographic subgroups (atop the documented 51M/9F cohort skew) is unexamined.
  • Reproducibility contract
    Extended by this week's practice: comparisons load saved folds and must pass an anchor-reproduction check before being read. Verified exact across all 60 folds this week.

The handoff to Week 9 names the two questions that matter most, in order. One: who are the 39 still-missed low-vigilant videos? Slice them by subject, by landmark quality, by drift-trajectory shape. Concentration in identifiable slices would be a fairness and deployment-scope finding; scatter would suggest irreducible midpoint ambiguity and argue for widening the middle band in the user-facing output. Two: does the session-baseline assumption fail dangerously? The truncation probe from Section 7 makes that measurable without new data.
