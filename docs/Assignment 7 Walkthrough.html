Section 1The assignment, and the project it lives inside

Every assignment in this course is a slice of one long project. So before we look at what changed this week, it's worth being clear about two separate things that are easy to blur together: what Assignment 7 asks of you, and what the project is trying to do. The assignment is the frame; the project is the picture.

The project is called Sleepy Sentinel. The goal is modest and personal: given a short clip of someone at their webcam, guess whether they look alert, low-vigilant, or drowsy. Not a medical device, not a driver-safety system — a self-monitoring aid, where the worst case of a wrong answer is a mistimed nudge to take a break. That framing matters, because it sets how careful we need to be and what counts as "good enough."

The three states are ordered. Alert, then low-vigilant, then drowsy: a slide from wide awake toward asleep. This ordering is not decoration. It changes what a mistake costs. Confusing alert with low-vigilant is a small slip; confusing alert with drowsy is a serious one. Hold onto that idea — it comes back the moment we start measuring success.

Definition · Ordinal target The label y ∈ {0, 1, 2} is ordinal: the classes have a natural order (alert < low-vigilant < drowsy), but the gaps between them aren't guaranteed to be equal. This sits between a plain category (no order) and a real number (order and measured distance).

The graded skill this week is judgment — making a defensible model choice and saying honestly what the evidence does and doesn't support. Section 2From video to a table of numbers

The raw material of this project is video — about 30 hours of it, roughly 111 gigabytes, sixty people each recorded three times. You cannot feed that to a logistic regression, and you would not want to. The first and most consequential design decision in the whole project happens before any model is trained: turning moving pictures into a tidy table of numbers.

Think of it as a funnel. A video is a flipbook of frames. Each frame gets a face read off it. Those per-frame readings are noisy and fast, so we summarize them over short windows of time. And each window becomes one row in a spreadsheet, with about seventeen columns. That table is what every model in this project — the floors, PERCLOS, the MLP, and this week's new contender — actually sees.

The workhorse in the second box is MediaPipe's face landmarker — a pretrained model from Google that finds a mesh of facial points and a set of "blendshape" scores (how open the jaw is, how closed each eye is) on a single face. Notice what it gives us: not pixels, but geometry. Where the eyelids are. How the jaw hangs. Which way the head tilts. This choice will turn out to matter enormously in Part B, so tuck it away: the project already runs a pretrained vision model — it just consumes the model's geometric outputs rather than raw images.

From that geometry we compute a handful of intuitive per-frame signals. The most important is the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) — the height of the eye opening divided by its width. When your eyes are wide open, EAR is large; as your lids droop, it shrinks toward zero. A blink is a brief dip; drowsiness is EAR staying low for longer stretches.

Definition · PERCLOS PERcentage of eye CLOSure — the fraction of time within a window that the eyes are more closed than a threshold. It is the single most established drowsiness signal in the literature, and in this project a one-feature model built on it is the reference every other model has to beat.

Now the subtle part: a single frame is a terrible unit of analysis. At any instant your eyes might just be mid-blink. So instead of asking "is this frame drowsy," we slide a window over the video — a few seconds of frames — and summarize the behavior inside it. From one window we compute things like the average EAR, its variability (ear_std), how often blinks happen (blink_rate), how long the longest blink lasted (blink_dur_max), how many yawns occurred, and how much the head drifted. Roughly seventeen such summaries make up one row.

Here is a caricature of what a single row looks like for a wide-awake window versus a drowsy one. Real values are normalized and standardized, but the direction of each difference is what your intuition should latch onto:

# One window = one row. (Illustrative values, not raw dataset numbers.)
                    alert window      drowsy window
  perclos             0.08              0.62      # eyes closed 8% vs 62% of the time
  ear_mean            0.29              0.14      # lids more open when alert
  ear_std             0.03              0.11      # droop-and-jerk makes EAR wobble
  blink_rate          0.20              0.55      # more frequent, heavier blinks
  blink_dur_max       0.12              0.80      # longest blink is much longer
  yawn_count          0                 2
  frac_head_down      0.02              0.35      # the nod
  label               0 (alert)         2 (drowsy)

Two columns in that neighborhood are deliberately held out of the models: brightness (bright_mean, warmth) and a data-quality flag (frac_face_missing). The brightness columns exist only to build a "trap" baseline — more on that in the next section — and the quality flag is a diagnostic, not a fatigue cue. Keeping them out of the real models is a rule written into the project charter, and honoring it will matter when we write this week's code.

Edge case · Missing faces are not closed eyes If MediaPipe fails to find a face in a frame (someone looks away, the lighting dies), that frame is marked missing — never silently treated as "eyes closed." A window with more than 30% missing frames is dropped entirely; short gaps under about a third of a second are interpolated. Confusing "no data" with "eyes shut" would manufacture fake drowsiness.

Why go to all this trouble instead of feeding video straight into a neural network? Three reasons, each of which the project can now defend with evidence. It collapses 111 GB into a 2 MB table you can experiment on in seconds. It strips away appearance — skin tone, background, camera — keeping only motion geometry, which guards against a model that secretly learns faces instead of fatigue. And it makes every feature something you can name and reason about, so when a model works you can say why. The cost, which we'll confront honestly in Section 6, is that squeezing time into per-window averages may discard exactly the temporal pattern that distinguishes true drowsiness.

Section 3Honest evaluation: leakage, LOSO, and the metrics

Here is a way to fool yourself into thinking you have a great model. Take your sixty people, pour all their windows into one pile, shuffle, and split off some for testing. Your accuracy will look wonderful — and it will be a lie. The reason is that windows from the same person are deeply alike. If some of Alice's windows train the model and others test it, the model can succeed by recognizing Alice, not by recognizing drowsiness. Come deployment, facing a stranger, it collapses.

Key concept · Subject leakage When data from the same individual appears in both training and test, a model can score well by memorizing the individual rather than learning the target concept. The defense is to split by person, never by row — so no one appears on both sides.

The strictest honest test of "will this work on someone new?" is Leave-One-Subject-Out cross-validation, or LOSO. Train on 59 people; test on the 1 you held out; write down the score. Then do it again holding out a different person. Sixty times over, until every subject has taken exactly one turn as the unseen stranger. Your reported number is the average across those sixty trials, and the spread across them tells you how much it depends on who the stranger is.

LOSO buys honesty at a price. Each held-out subject has only three videos, so each fold's score is quantized and jumpy, and the standard deviation across folds is large — you'll see bands like ± 0.38 next to a mean of 0.48. That width is not a flaw in the method; it is an accurate confession that with sixty people, you genuinely cannot tell close models apart with confidence. Keeping that humility in view is part of the graded skill.

Two more leaks are sealed off in code, and it is worth knowing they exist because this week's experiment inherits them for free. The StandardScaler that puts features on a common scale is fit only on the training people, then applied to the test person — otherwise the test data would whisper its statistics into training. And per-subject EAR normalization uses only that subject's own frames, never their alert video as a cheat-sheet baseline that wouldn't exist at deployment.

Scoring an ordinal problem

Now, how do we turn predictions into a number? Plain accuracy — fraction correct — is tempting but blind to the ordering we cared about in Section 1. To accuracy, calling a drowsy clip "alert" is exactly as wrong as calling it "low-vigilant." But we agreed those mistakes are not equal. We need a metric that knows the classes live on a line.

Definition · Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) QWK is the project's headline metric. It scores agreement between predicted and true ordinal labels while penalizing mistakes by the square of how many steps off they are: a one-step miss costs a little, a two-step miss (alert↔drowsy) costs four times as much. It's scaled so that 1.0 is perfect, 0.0 is no better than chance guessing, and negative is worse than chance.

A companion metric, rank-MAE, is the average number of steps by which predictions miss — lower is better, and it reads in the same units as the labels (a rank-MAE of 0.57 means "off by about half a class on average"). Where QWK punishes big misses disproportionately, rank-MAE reports the plain average distance. Watching both keeps you honest: a model can nudge one without the other.

One last mechanical step ties the two halves of the funnel together. Models predict per window, but the thing we actually care about is the video. So for each video we average the window-level class probabilities and take the most likely class as the video's prediction. Scores are always computed at the video level — 180 videos, three per held-out subject — because that's the real unit of the decision.

# window predictions  ->  one prediction per video
video 03/10  windows: [drowsy, drowsy, alert, drowsy, low-vig, drowsy, ...]
             mean prob = {alert: 0.18, low-vig: 0.22, drowsy: 0.60}
             => predicted label = drowsy   # argmax of the averaged probabilities

With this scaffolding — split by person, seal the leaks, score on the ordinal line, aggregate to videos — we finally have a fair courtroom. Any model we drop in is judged by exactly the same rules as every model before it. That is precisely what makes this week's comparison trustworthy, so let's introduce the new contender.

Section 4The experiment: adding one honest contender

We've now assembled everything the experiment stands on: a table of geometric features, a fair courtroom that splits by person and scores on the ordinal line, and a leaderboard where PERCLOS-only quietly leads. Assignment 6 ended by writing down the single next move, almost as a dare:

The Assignment 6 dare "Test whether the full feature set can beat PERCLOS with a simpler, interpretable tabular model, using the exact same LOSO folds. If it cannot beat PERCLOS, keep PERCLOS."

Read that carefully, because it quietly reframes the puzzle. Last week's loser was a neural net — a model with plenty of capacity. The natural reflex after a neural net underperforms is to reach for a bigger one. Assignment 6 refuses that reflex. It asks instead: what if the MLP's problem was never too little capacity, but too much — sixty subjects is thin data, and a flexible model overfits thin data. A plain multinomial logistic regression on the same seventeen features has almost no capacity to overfit. If it can beat PERCLOS where the MLP couldn't, the lesson is about the right kind of model for the data, not the right size.

Definition · Multinomial logistic regression A linear model that, for each of the three classes, learns one weight per feature, then turns the weighted sums into class probabilities. No hidden layers, no nonlinearity — just "how much does each feature push toward each class." Its transparency is the point: after training you can read the weights and see what it relies on (Section 6).

How the code is structured — and why the change is small

The nicest thing about a well-built pipeline is that a new experiment costs almost no new code. The training loop doesn't know or care what a "baseline" is; it just asks each model to produce, for every window, three class probabilities. Everything after that — aggregating windows into videos, scoring, saving — is shared. So a baseline is described not by a class or a subclass, but by a tiny record: a name, and the list of feature columns it's allowed to look at.

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class BaselineSpec:
    name: str
    feature_columns: tuple[str, ...] = ()

PERCLOS-only is just BaselineSpec("perclos", ("perclos",)) — one feature. Luminance-only is the same idea pointed at the brightness columns. The floor model, majority, has an empty feature list and simply predicts the most common class. The experiment this week is, at heart, one more line in the list of available baselines: the same logistic machinery, but handed every legitimate feature instead of one.

# src/training/baselines.py  — the one meaningful addition
full_feature_columns = tuple(
    col for col in windows_df.columns
    if col not in NON_MODEL_INPUT_COLUMNS
)
if len(full_feature_columns) > 1:
    baselines.append(BaselineSpec("logistic_full", full_feature_columns))

Notice the guard: NON_MODEL_INPUT_COLUMNS. This is where Section 2's charter rule becomes code. That set names everything the full-feature model must not see — the identifiers, the label, the data-quality flag, and crucially the four luminance columns reserved for the trap baseline. Getting this right is what makes the comparison fair: logistic_full gets the eighteen legitimate fatigue features, and not one brightness column that would let it cheat the way the luminance baseline is designed to catch.

Edge case · A quiet bug this surfaced Writing the explicit exclusion set revealed that last week's MLP never applied it — its feature path excluded only IDs and the label, so it silently trained on the four luminance columns too (22 features, not the charter's 18). Because luminance-only scores near chance, the practical effect is likely harmless noise rather than leakage — but it means the A6 MLP comparison carries a caveat, and it's flagged in the memo's blocker section to fix before any future neural run. Finding this is itself a Part A deliverable: "identify one blocker or uncertainty in your modeling process."

Everything else the run needs, it already had. The same folds.json, the same seed of 42, the same train-only scaler, the same window-to-video aggregation. Because the logistic path runs inside scikit-learn with a fixed seed, it is fully deterministic: run it twice, get identical numbers. And as a built-in sanity check, re-running regenerated the majority, luminance, and PERCLOS rows exactly as Assignment 6 reported them — proof that nothing in the shared pipeline drifted, and that any change we see in the leaderboard is really the new model, not a moving floor.

To run it is a single command, and the new contender appears alongside the old floors:

uv run train_alertness
# Runs: majority, perclos, luminance, logistic_full
# Writes metrics, confusion matrices, and diagnostics into outputs/

So the entire "experiment," in terms of code, is a handful of lines plus a carefully chosen exclusion set. That is exactly as it should be. The intellectual work went into the question — capacity versus representation — and the pipeline was built so that asking a sharp question doesn't require a sprawling change. Section 5The results: did it beat PERCLOS?

Yes — and cleanly enough to be worth stating plainly, cautiously enough to be worth qualifying honestly. On every headline metric, the full-feature logistic regression edged out the PERCLOS-only baseline that had led since Assignment 6, and it did so with a fold band no wider than PERCLOS's. Here is the whole leaderboard, this week's entry in green:

The QWK gain is +0.071 (0.481 versus 0.410), with a band of ± 0.378 that is actually a touch narrower than PERCLOS's ± 0.414. Rank-MAE dropped from 0.617 to 0.567 — predictions land, on average, half a step closer to the truth. And it clears the A6 MLP on all four numbers while being far simpler, which is the real vindication of Assignment 6's hunch: the seventeen features do carry signal beyond PERCLOS; the MLP just wasn't the right instrument to draw it out.

Key concept · The honest caveat that comes with the win A higher mean is not automatically a real improvement when fold bands are this wide. So rather than lean on the average alone, look at the models fold by fold: across the 60 held-out subjects, logistic_full beat PERCLOS in 18, tied in 31, and lost in 11. More wins than losses, but a great many ties — the improvement is real and consistent in direction, not a landslide. That is the correct way to describe it.

Where the improvement actually lives

Averages hide anatomy. To see what got better, pool all 180 video predictions into a confusion matrix — true state down the side, predicted state across the top. The green diagonal is correct calls; the red corners are the dangerous alert↔drowsy confusions QWK punishes most.

Three things jump out. Drowsy recall rose (31→35 of 60 correct): the model catches more genuinely tired clips, which is the whole point of an early-fatigue aid. The most dangerous error — a drowsy person confidently called alert — fell from 19 to 13. Summed across both corners, two-step errors dropped from 24 to 17 out of 180, a 29% reduction in exactly the mistakes that matter most. The small price is two alert videos that slipped to a neighboring class (48→46), a one-step error QWK barely penalizes.

Edge case · The class that refuses to budge Look at the middle row. Low-vigilant recall is stuck at 14 of 60 — exactly where PERCLOS was, barely above chance. Every model tried so far fails on the ordinal midpoint. This is not a rounding artifact; it is the project's most stubborn failure mode, and Section 6 will argue it hints at what representation the data is really missing.

So the verdict Part A reports is disciplined: by the charter's own rule — the full feature set must beat PERCLOS on QWK or on the dangerous corner errors to earn its complexity — logistic_full qualifies, improving both, and becomes the new leading candidate. Stated modestly, because the bands overlap and half the folds tie. That combination of a real result and an honest hedge is precisely the "makes a defensible model choice" the assignment grades. But a good scientist doesn't stop at "it won" — they ask why it won. That question is the whole of the next section.

Section 6Opening the box: what the model actually leans on

A linear model has a rare virtue: you can ask it, directly, what it pays attention to. Each feature carries a weight for each class, and because every feature was standardized to the same scale before training, the size of a weight is a fair measure of how much that feature moves the decision. Big weight, big influence. This is the payoff for choosing an interpretable model over a neural net — and it turns "it won" into "it won because."

To read the weights honestly, a small companion script (scripts/inspect_logistic_full.py) refits the exact same pipeline on each of the 60 LOSO training folds and averages the absolute weights across them. Averaging over folds matters: a feature that looks important in one fold but flips sign in another is not a stable signal. Here is what carries the decision, largest first:

This picture is the intellectual heart of the whole week, so it's worth reading slowly. PERCLOS is still the single loudest voice — no surprise; it was a strong model by itself. But it is not a solo. Beneath it sits a tight cluster of blink-dynamics features — how long the longest blink lasted, how heavy the average blink is, how frequently blinks come, how much the eye-opening wobbles. Together they carry real, stable weight. Meanwhile the yawn, jaw, and head-pose features barely move the needle.

Key concept · Why logistic_full beats PERCLOS The extra win over PERCLOS-only does not come from the whole feature set pulling together, and it does not come from yawning or head-nodding. It comes almost entirely from eyelid temporal dynamics — the fine-grained texture of blinking that a single PERCLOS number can't capture. The full-feature model is essentially "PERCLOS, plus a richer description of how the eyes are behaving."

Now connect this to the failure mode from Section 5. The low-vigilant middle class stays stuck at chance for every model. And here we can see a plausible reason. All of the signal lives in eyelid behavior summarized within a window — averages and counts. But low vigilance may not be a matter of how much the eyes close on average; it may be a matter of how that behavior drifts over time — the slow slide from crisp blinks to heavy ones. Per-window averages, by construction, throw that drift away. This is exactly the cost Section 2 warned about, now with evidence pointing at it.

Definition · The temporal hypothesis The remaining bottleneck may be representational, not architectural: the tabularization preserved within-window eyelid statistics but discarded how they evolve across windows. Notably, the dataset's own published baseline (Ghoddoosian et al., 2019) found its signal precisely in sequential blink features fed to a recurrent network — independent support for the same idea.

One more diagnostic closes the loop, and it tempers the celebration. How confident is the model when it's right versus wrong? A useful warning system should be surer when correct. logistic_full averages 0.489 confidence on correct calls and 0.446 on incorrect ones — a gap, and slightly wider than PERCLOS's, but far too narrow to hang a "take a break now" alarm on. Calibration remains unsolved, and the memo says so.

So interpretation gives us something more valuable than a trophy: a direction. The gain is real and it is made of eyelid dynamics; the wall is the middle class; and the most promising next move is to stop averaging time away. That directly shapes the forward-looking half of the assignment — which is where Part B picks up.

Section 7Part B: is transfer learning even relevant here?

Week 7 taught convolutional networks, pretrained vision models, and transfer learning — taking a network trained on millions of generic images and repurposing it for your task. Part B asks the disciplined question: does any of that belong in this project? The tempting answer, and the wrong one, is "yes, let's bolt a CNN on." The assignment is practically engineered to reward resisting that temptation with a reasoned decision.

The verdict here is a careful middle: indirectly relevant. Not "yes, adopt it," and not a flat "no, irrelevant." To see why, recall the single most important sentence from Section 2.

Key concept · The project already does transfer learning MediaPipe's face landmarker is a pretrained, CNN-based vision model. The project already runs it and consumes its outputs — it just uses geometric features (eyelid position, jaw openness) instead of generic embeddings. So the Week 7 idea of "use a frozen pretrained vision model as a feature extractor" isn't hypothetical here; it is the foundation the whole pipeline already stands on.

That reframing is what makes "indirectly relevant" the precise answer. The core concept from lecture is present and load-bearing. What is not warranted is going further — swapping the geometric features for raw-pixel CNNs or fine-tuning an image model end-to-end. And the reasons are exactly the evidence gathered in Parts A and earlier weeks:

  • The charter scopes out raw-pixel modeling, and that scope hasn't become infeasible — the geometric approach is working and improving.
  • Sixty subjects is far too few to fine-tune a large image model without it memorizing faces — the very subject-leakage risk Section 3 built the whole evaluation to prevent.
  • Capacity is not the bottleneck. Section 6 showed a low-capacity linear model beating a neural net; dropping in high-dimensional generic embeddings would worsen the capacity-to-data mismatch, not fix it.
  • Pixels would reopen the confound the geometry closes. The luminance baseline near chance is evidence the current design isn't a brightness detector; raw-pixel models would let lighting, background, and appearance leak back in.

The honest scientist also names what would change the decision, and here the assignment's forward-looking purpose kicks in — Part B is setting up Assignment 8. There are two candidate tests, and a real-world constraint decides their priority:

Edge case · Feasibility is part of the argument The purest transfer-learning test — frozen embeddings versus geometric features — would settle the question definitively. But the raw videos were deliberately discarded after extraction (that was the whole point of going tabular; keeping 111 GB was never the plan). Re-acquiring them costs many hours and may not fit on disk. A good engineering decision weighs a test's expected value against its cost — and Section 6's evidence suggests the bottleneck is temporal, not appearance-based, which lowers the expected payoff of the expensive test. So it stays on the list, but second.

Finally, Part B updates the project's risk ledger. Depending on a pretrained model isn't free even when you only use its geometry: MediaPipe's own training data is undocumented, so its per-subgroup reliability (glasses, facial hair, skin tone) is an unquantified failure mode that compounds the dataset's known demographic skew (51 men, 9 women). Naming that honestly — rather than pretending the pretrained dependency is risk-free — is the kind of responsible-AI accounting the assignment's Part B explicitly asks for.
