Section 1The autopsy week: what Assignment 9 actually asks

Every assignment so far has ended with a scoreboard: floors in A6, a champion in A7, a better champion in A8. Assignment 9 inverts the question. The professor's framing rules out the obvious temptation up front: this is not a new sequence-modeling build. The Week 9 lecture covers RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, and attention — but only so you can decide, in one part of one memo, whether those ideas are risks or tools for your project. The main work happens in the evidence you already have.

The memo has five parts, and they form a pipeline of their own: snapshot where the project stands (A), find concrete failure patterns and trace them to the people affected (B), check those failures against the risks you predicted back in the Assignment 4 audit (C), decide whether time and attention concepts matter here (D), and hand Week 10 a table of decision-ready evidence rows (E).

The grading emphasis contains one phrase worth taping to the monitor: evidence-first. "Do not only say that the model is biased, risky, or weak; show the model behavior that supports the claim." A failure analysis that says "the middle class is hard" is a vibe. One that says "the 39 missed low-vigilant videos split 25 toward alert and 14 toward drowsy, the two groups differ in their starting eyelid levels by a factor of 2.4, and the model is more confident on the misses than the hits" is evidence. This week's job is turning the first kind of sentence into the second kind.

Definition · Failure analysis A failure analysis decomposes an aggregate score into named patterns of error — slices, subgroups, conditions, directions, time periods — each backed by concrete model behavior, each connected to a likely cause and a stakeholder consequence. The aggregate says how often you're wrong; the failure analysis says when, about whom, in which direction, and how sure you sounded.

Section 2Slicing the misses: who are the 39 low-vigilant failures?

Start with what we knew on Monday: the champion recognizes 21 of 60 low-vigilant videos. That number was A8's headline improvement (up from 14), and also its loudest remaining complaint — the early-warning class is still the one the model gets wrong most. A9's first question is the natural next one: are the 39 misses a crowd or a pattern?

The method is the workhorse of all failure analysis: take the champion's saved per-video predictions, keep only the true-low-vigilant rows, and join on everything we know about each video that might explain its fate. Four covariate families were chosen, each encoding a specific suspicion:

Now the second finding, which is the uncomfortable one. Look back at the confidence column. The model averages 0.437 confidence when it gets low-vigilant right — and 0.512 / 0.614 when it gets it wrong, with individual misses reaching 0.92. Confidence rises as the video drifts away from the ambiguous middle toward a clean-looking (but incorrect) neighboring profile. The errors aren't hesitant errors. They're the model's most assured statements about this class.

Key idea · Overconfident errors are worse than frequent ones A user can live with an aid that says "not sure" often. What breaks trust is an aid that's most certain precisely when it's wrong. For the class this project exists to catch early, higher confidence currently signals a higher chance of error — which is why Part B rates this failure pattern's severity by its confidence behavior, not just its count.

The third finding is a negative, and negatives deserve equal billing: landmark quality is exonerated. Mean frac_face_missing is 0.0005–0.0006 across hits and both miss groups — indistinguishable, and tiny. The misses are not videos where MediaPipe lost the face. Likewise, misses scatter across 39 different subjects, and a subject missing their low-vigilant video is barely more likely to also miss their drowsy one (15/39 vs 10/21). No convenient "it's just bad subjects" story survives contact with the table.

Definition · Negative evidence A slicing analysis that finds no difference between groups is not a failed analysis — it eliminates a candidate cause. Ruling out landmark quality moves the likely explanation toward the label itself: KSS 6–7 ("low-vigilant") genuinely sits between its neighbors, and some sessions live near a boundary for their entire recording. That distinction — quality problem vs. inherent ambiguity — changes what Week 10 should recommend.

What the evidence cannot settle is whether the remaining misses are irreducible midpoint ambiguity or a representation still missing some cue — the dataset publishes only the 3-way class, not each video's exact KSS score, so we can't check whether missed-as-alert videos were the KSS-6 end and missed-as-drowsy the KSS-7 end. That gap goes in the memo's "missing evidence" column, where honest analyses put the things they wish they knew.

Section 3Corners and confidence: the errors that matter most

The charter decided long ago which errors are unforgivable: the corners, where a truly alert video is called drowsy or — much worse for this use case — a truly drowsy video is called alert. Adjacent confusion is the cost of doing business on an ordinal scale; corner mass is the failure that would make the aid worse than nothing. A7 reported 17 corner errors out of 180 videos. A8 reported… also 17. Case closed, nothing moved?

This is where aggregate thinking quietly lies to you. Seventeen equals seventeen, but a count is not a set. The corner-migration analysis pivots the two models' predictions side by side per video and asks which specific videos occupy the corner under each:

anchor_corner = (wide["label"] - wide["pred_logistic_full_anchor"]).abs() == 2
trend_corner  = (wide["label"] - wide["pred_trend_logistic"]).abs() == 2

corners["category"] = np.select(
    [anchor_corner & trend_corner, anchor_corner],
    ["persistent", "fixed_by_trends"],
    default="introduced_by_trends",
)

The answer: only 13 of the 17 are the same videos. Four true-drowsy videos that the anchor dumped in the alert corner were pulled out by the trend features (04/10, 06/10, 09/10, 16/10 — three rescued to low-vigilant, one all the way to correct). And four new corners appeared, including 53/10, a drowsy video the anchor classified correctly that the trend model now calls alert.

Direction matters as much as membership. Of the champion's 17 corners, 11 are drowsy-called-alert and 6 are alert-called-drowsy. For a fatigue aid these are not symmetric mistakes: alert-called-drowsy is an annoying false alarm; drowsy-called-alert is the aid staying silent while its user is most impaired — a silent failure at 6% of all videos (11/180). This single number does more to cap what the project can responsibly claim than any aggregate metric in the portfolio.

Edge case · Stability of a count ≠ stability of behavior Whenever two models tie on a summary statistic, ask whether they tie on the instances. Equal counts with churning membership means the decision boundary moved through a populated region — some cases crossed in each direction. That's a materially different (and less comforting) situation than a boundary that left everything untouched, and you can only see it by joining predictions per instance.

Confidence, conditioned on what the model is about to do

The second half of this section revisits A8's calibration note with a sharper lens. A8 reported one aggregate gap: mean confidence 0.575 on correct videos vs 0.520 on incorrect — small but positive. The A9 analysis conditions on predicted class instead, and the reason is stakeholder-shaped: a warning gate doesn't experience "average confidence." It experiences confidence at the moment the model proposes a specific label. The question that matters is: when the model says drowsy, how much should we believe it — and does its confidence distinguish its right calls from its wrong ones?

It's the top action row in Part E.

Key idea · Condition diagnostics on the action, not the average Diagnostics should be sliced the way decisions will be made. A deployed aid acts per prediction, so calibration must hold per predicted class. An aggregate that pools three classes can hide a sign flip in exactly the class your product exists to serve — which is precisely what happened here.

Section 4The cold-start probe: testing a blind spot the dataset can't show

Assignment 8 introduced the drift features and, in the same breath, confessed their structural weakness. drift measures each window's departure from the session's own start. So a user who opens the aid already drowsy has drift ≈ 0 on precisely the features that flag drowsiness — the session's start is their fatigued state, so nothing has "drifted." The feared consequence: the aid fails hardest on the person who needs it most, and the dataset can never reveal this, because every UTA-RLDD video holds one constant label from first frame to last. There are no recordings of someone becoming drowsy mid-session, and none that begin mid-episode.

When your data cannot exhibit a failure mode, you have two honest options: declare it untested, or simulate it and be explicit about the simulation's limits. A8's handoff proposed the simulation: score sessions that are truncated to begin partway through a recording. A drowsy video with its first quarter removed is a crude but fair stand-in for "the user opened the aid already fatigued" — the model meets elevated eyelid dynamics with no calm baseline to compare against.

← removed: first 25% (the "warm-up" the model never sees)session as the model sees it →

The implementation has three load-bearing decisions, each easy to get subtly wrong:

First: truncate the test videos only. The question is how the deployed model — trained once, on full sessions — behaves when a user starts mid-episode. Truncating training data would answer a different question (could we train on truncated sessions?) and would also break the anchor comparison. So each fold's model is fit exactly as in A8, and only its test subject's windows are cut.

Second: restart the clock at the cut. It is not enough to drop rows. The kept windows are renumbered from zero so that elapsed_min, the rolling deltas, and the expanding drift baseline all treat the cut point as the session's beginning. Skip the renumbering and the recomputed features would quietly remember the deleted past:

def truncate_video_windows(windows_df, fraction):
    """Drop the first `fraction` of each video's windows and restart the clock."""
    ordered = windows_df.sort_values(["video_id", "window_idx"]).copy()
    position = ordered.groupby("video_id").cumcount()
    video_sizes = ordered.groupby("video_id")["window_idx"].transform("size")
    kept = ordered[position >= np.floor(video_sizes * fraction).astype(int)].copy()
    kept["window_idx"] = kept.groupby("video_id").cumcount()  # the cut is the new t=0
    return kept.reset_index(drop=True)

A unit test pins the property that matters: after truncation + trend recomputation, every drift and delta feature at the first kept window is exactly 0, and elapsed_min is 0 — the model has genuinely forgotten the removed history.

Third: prove the probe reproduces the champion before reading anything else. This is A8's anchor discipline applied again. The probe includes truncation fraction 0.0 — no cut at all — and its 180 video predictions must match the saved A8 champion run. They do: every predicted label identical, probabilities within 5×10⁻³ (the analysis ran under a different sklearn/Python version than A8; the graded check in a9_manifest.json records both facts, and a re-run in the pinned uv environment is expected to match to solver precision). Only after that check do the truncated numbers mean anything.

Definition · Reproduction anchor Before comparing a new run against saved evidence, re-run a configuration whose answer you already know and confirm it matches. If the known case reproduces, differences in the unknown cases are attributable to your intervention (here: truncation) rather than to drifted code, seeds, or environments. A8 used this to link its runs to A7; A9 uses it to link the probe to A8. The chain now spans A5→A9.

Two findings, and they point in opposite emotional directions.

The feared failure did not materialize. Drowsy recall is 35, 34, 35 across the three conditions — essentially flat. Deleting the calm warm-up does not blind the model to drowsiness, because the level features (blink durations, PERCLOS) carry that detection on their own. The model does not need to watch you deteriorate to call you drowsy; it can see long, slow blinks in absolute terms. The worst-case story from A8 — "the aid fails exactly on the already-drowsy user" — is, on this simulation, wrong. That's worth saying plainly, because failure analyses are allowed to deliver good news when the evidence says so.

But the A8 gain turns out to live entirely in the warm-up. Low-vigilant recall drops from 21 to 14 at 25% truncation — and 14/60 is precisely the A7 anchor's number. Cut the first quarter of the session and the trend features stop helping the one class they were adopted to help; the champion degrades to last assignment's model exactly where this assignment's improvement was claimed. The middle class, we now know from Section 2, is discriminated by trajectory relative to baseline — and trajectory-relative-to-baseline is the very thing a cold start deletes.

A corroborating check needs no simulation at all: within ordinary, untruncated sessions, window-level accuracy in the first position decile is the worst for every class (alert 0.59 vs ≈0.70 mid-session; low-vigilant 0.27 vs ≈0.33 late; drowsy 0.48 vs ≈0.55). Early-session predictions are measurably weaker in the real data, not just under surgery. The deployment consequence writes itself: the aid's first minutes are reduced-accuracy, the user must be told, and sessions should ideally begin near one's alert baseline — an assumption that now sits, named and quantified, in the audit trail.

Edge case · The simulation's own fine print The probe truncates acted, constant-label recordings. It cannot manufacture a session that genuinely transitions alert→drowsy mid-recording, and the non-monotonic wobble (25% slightly worse than 50%) is within noise at 60 videos per class — the memo declines to interpret it. Simulated evidence earns a Medium-High confidence tag in Part E, not High: strong enough to require a deployment caveat, too weak to fully settle the blind spot.

Section 5What the lecture was for: sequences, attention, and the decision card

Week 9's lecture walks through recurrent models and attention — hidden states, LSTM gates, GRU gates, encoder-decoder attention with its score/normalize/read loop. None of it asks to be implemented. Its role in Assignment 9 is stranger and more useful: it hands you a vocabulary of risks, then asks whether your project speaks that language. Part D is a seven-field decision card; this section walks the concepts and then fills the card.

The concepts, compressed to what this project needs

Order changes the problem. The moment rows stop being exchangeable — the moment "which came before" carries information — random shuffling becomes a lie and evaluation must respect time. Our windows have been ordered all along (that's what window_idx is), and A8's trend features are exactly a wager that order matters. It paid: the only middle-class improvement in five assignments came from features describing how a window compares to its session's past.

Recurrent models carry state. An RNN reuses one learned update across positions, threading a hidden state ht forward as compressed memory; LSTMs and GRUs add learned gates that control what's kept, written, and exposed, taming the vanishing-gradient problem over long spans. This project met that family personally in A8: a small GRU read each video's full window sequence and lost to the linear model on every metric, early-stopping at a median of epoch 6 — the unmistakable signature of a sequence model memorizing 150 training videos rather than learning from them.

Attention builds a per-step context. Instead of forcing a whole input sequence into one final state, attention keeps every encoder annotation, then — at each output step — scores them against the decoder's state, normalizes the scores with a softmax, and reads a weighted sum. The lecture's worked example (indexed retrieval) shows both its power (0.98 token accuracy vs 0.17 without attention) and its trap: on sequences longer than training, the model still attends — confidently — to the wrong positions. Attention improves the information pathway; it does not guarantee the pathway is used correctly.

Key idea · Attention-based overinterpretation Attention weights look like explanations — bright cells on a heatmap, "the model looked here." The lecture's long-sequence failure case is the antidote: a model can attend to the wrong thing while producing a beautiful, confident map. Reading meaning into attention weights fit on tiny data is a named responsible-AI risk in this assignment, and it's one reason the decision card below declines the whole model family at n=60 subjects.

Temporal leakage makes evaluation lie. The lecture's checklist: What is the prediction time? Which features exist by then? Which entities repeat across records? A random split over time-ordered data lets future-derived clues into training; repeated entities let identity stand in for signal. This project's versions of those questions were answered in earlier weeks — but Part D requires saying so explicitly, with evidence.

The interesting subtlety is the last-but-one row. Rejecting the GRU was empirical — it ran and lost. Rejecting attention is inferential — it never ran. Is that legitimate? The argument has three legs: attention-augmented models strictly add parameters to the sequence family that just failed for having too many; every added-capacity model across this whole project (MLP in A6, GRU in A8) has lost to a linear model on a better representation; and the temporal signal the data does contain proved simple enough for hand-built trends to capture. Testing attention would cost real time to answer a question whose every available signpost points the same way — and Part D's job is exactly this kind of justified triage, not reflexive experimentation.

Notice also what the card does not say. It doesn't say "time is irrelevant" — the opposite: time mattered enough to change the champion and to create this week's headline risk (warm-up dependence). The lecture's dichotomy — is your project sequential or not? — turns out to have a third answer this project embodies: the signal is temporal, but the model needn't be. Causal feature engineering moved the time-structure into columns a linear model can read, keeping interpretability and statistical honesty at a data scale where sequence learners drown.

Section 6Parts C and E: tracing risks and handing off to Week 10

Part B established what fails. Part C asks a more reflective question: did we predict it? The mechanism is a trace — pick prior audit risks, assumptions, and success criteria, then classify each against this week's evidence using a five-word vocabulary: confirmed, weakened, revised, still untested, or newly discovered. The point isn't bookkeeping. A project whose failures were all foreseen in its audit has a working risk process; one whose failures are all surprises does not. This project turns out to be mostly — not entirely — the first kind.

Two traces deserve a closer look, because they show the vocabulary doing real work.

The session-baseline trace is "revised," not "confirmed" — and the difference is the finding. A8 predicted a specific failure: drift-blindness on already-drowsy users. The probe found the prediction half-right. The mechanism is real (drift features do go silent at a cold start) but the consequence landed elsewhere — drowsy detection survives on level features; it's the middle class that pays. Classifying this as "revised based on new evidence" records that the risk model itself was corrected, which is exactly the audit-maintenance behavior the assignment is trying to teach.

The cohort-skew trace is "still untested" — with a sharper reason than last time. Earlier memos could say subgroup analysis "hadn't been done yet." This week's version is stronger: it cannot be done from current artifacts, because the feature table deliberately carries no demographic columns, and producing them means manually annotating raw videos that stay local by privacy design. An honest "untested" with a named obstacle caps the recommendation scope (personal use, single user) far more credibly than a hand-wave — evidence of absence of evidence, as it were.

Definition · The deployment-judgment vocabulary Week 10 will place this project on a five-point scale: deploy, revise, limit to decision support, do not use, or too uncertain to judge. "Limit to decision support" means the system may inform a human's judgment but must not act autonomously (no auto-firing warnings) and must not be relied on where being wrong is unrecoverable. Part E's job is to stock that judgment with rows of evidence — for and against — so Week 10 argues from a table, not a feeling.

Why the refactor earns its diff

A8's memo made a claim in passing: "the champion path remains sklearn-only; torch stays optional for rejected/diagnostic runs." This week that claim got audited by reality — the failure analysis needs the trend-feature code but has no business importing a neural-network library — and reality found it was only morally true. temporal.py imported torch at module level for two residents (the GRU and its padding helper), so anything touching trend features dragged the whole torch stack along. The fix is an import-boundary move, not a rewrite: the two torch users now live in sequence_models.py, which only the A8 experiment runner imports.

Key idea · Dependency claims are architecture claims "This path only needs X" is only true if the import graph enforces it. Moving the torch users behind their own module makes the memo's claim checkable by running python -c "import training.failure_analysis" on a torch-less machine.
